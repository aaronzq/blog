Infrared light imaging using InGaAs detectors

For applications that require high sensitivity over the 900-1700 nm wavelength range, i.e. shortwave infrared (SWIR), InGaAs sensors are used. While silicon-based sensors have excellent sensitivity between UV and NIR, the bandgap of silicon prevent them from having sufficient sensitivity over 1100 nm. In contrast, InGaAs-based cameras have a lower bandgap, making this material the preferred technology for SWIR.

However, the intrinsic limitation of a lower bandgap is the noise performance. See Teledyne tutorial:

The biggest limitation of InGaAs cameras is their noise level. The lower bandgap of the material also produces higher dark current (thermally generated signal). Therefore, InGaAs cameras need to be deeply cooled to increase signal to noise by reducing dark noise as much as possible.

The table below exhibits the typical noise performance of InGaAs detectors. In reference, the scientific CMOS camera, such as Teledyne Kinetix, has extremely small readout noise (0.7 e) and low dark current (0.47 e/pixel/s).

InGaAs detectors in microscopy

Using longer wavelengths, such as SWIR/NIR-II, in optical microscopy provides deeper imaging depth in dense tissue thanks to less photon scattering. Fluorescence imaging in NIR-II window has achieved millimetre depths with spatial resolutions on the order of several micrometeres, representing a substantial improvement compared with the about 100 microns by visible light[1]. Studies have demonstrated mouse cerebral vasculatures through intact skull and scalp [2]. In this work, NIR-IIa region (1300~1400nm) was rationally chosen to both eliminate scattering below 1300 nm and to avoid incrased absorption in the >1400 nm region due to first overtone vibration of water molecules (see figure below). A liquid-nitrogen-cooled InGaAs array detector (320x256 px, Princeton OMA-V).

Widefield microscopy in SWIR range has demonstrated deep tissue imaging in a variety of applications [2]. The parallel detection using a 2D array delivers high imaging speed. However, the poor optical sectioning capability lower the image contrast and limits its usage in thin and sparse samples. Confocal scanning systems [5] rejects the out-of-focus light by a physical pinhole, making it more suitable for deep scattering tissue imaging. It has even demonstrated imaging depths of 1.3 mm through cranial windows in the brain [6]. To accelerate the image acquisition, line scanning can replace the point scanning strategy while maintaining optical sectioning ability [3] (see figure below). Here, an InGaAs line sensor will replace the photodiode or photomultiplier tube (PMT) and out-of-focus light is rejected by a physical slit.

The confocal systems require tedious scanning and are generally slower than widefield counterparts. First, the detectors at the SWIR wavelength are lack of high efficiency and prone to high noise. Additionally, the low quantum yield of available organic dyes and the long lifetimes of quantum dots further ask for a long pixel dwell time. Therefore, we rarely see SWIR confocal microscopy performing at video rate.

Dynamic imaging (e.g. video rate, >25fps ) in SWIR range with optical sectioning capability presents an unsolved challenge. From the survey on commercial detectors, the camera bandwidth does not seem to be the bottleneck for this challenge. Neither does the scanner, because even commercial confocal systems (such as Leica SP8) deliver video rate imaging using resonant scannner (at visible light wavelength). To achieve high-speed SWIR imaging, we really have to increase the detector’s sensitivity so that a small pixel dwell time is tolerable.

Within the confocal scanning framework, line scanning could be a better option since it parallely measures all pixels in one row. It lessens the trade-off between imaging speed and pixel dwell time. Besides, 1D sensor has a higher ceiling in imaging speed compared to 0D sensor, and even to 2D sensor. Thanks to less manufacture difficulty, a line detector is usually made in larger format. The Manx 2048 SQ, for example, records 1x2048 pixels at 256 kHz. By scanning in the orthogonal direction, it could achieve a pixel resolution of 2048x2048 at 125 Hz, which have not accomplished by any commercially avaible 2D InGaAs sensors (see detector list). However, it is without consideration of signal-to-noise ratio and the corresponding pixel exposure time (3.9 $\mu s$) presents a critical disadvantage to 2D sensor (8 ms, if performing at same framerate).

Line scanning is perferred for high-speed SWIR confocal microscopy. But to unlock its speed potential, the key is to collect sufficient signals to fight its detector noise.

P.S. There are alternative ways of optical sectioning [8]. Possibly optical sectioning could be achived without tedious confocal scanning.

Deep tissue imaging using ultrasound

Deep tissue imaging can alternatively be acomplished by acoustic imaging [4]:

Deep-tissue imaging with optical contrast can alternatively be accomplished with hybrid approaches combining light and sound. In particular, ultrasound waves experience little scattering in soft biological tissues as compared to light.

Photoacoustic (PA) imaging utilizes the photothermal mechanism in biological tissues where a laser pulse is absorbed to expand the target with heat. The acoustic wave is produced and then detected by transducer. The imaging depth at centimeter is possible when using near-infrared wavelength laser. However, the PA methods generally differ from the fluorescence-based techniques in that the image contrast is mainly associated with hemoglobin absorption, which may compromise sensitive detection of extrinsic labels in the presence of strong background absorption by blood.

Microbolometers

Microbolometers detects radiotion with wavelengths between 7.5-14 um, the LWIR range. The radiation heats it and changes its electrical resistance. The resistance change is then measured and processed. The current technology allows the pixel size and number to approximate semiconductor based photon detector. Howver, it is generally less sensitive (due to higher noise). The sensitivity is partly limited by the thermal conductance of the pixel. The speed of response it limited by the thermal heat capacity divided by the thermal conductance. Reducing the heat capacity increases the speed but also increases statistical mechanical thermal temperature fluctuations (noise).
