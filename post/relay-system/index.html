Notes about using optical relay in imaging system

Aaron
2024-07-01
optics

A relay system forms a conjugate plane that replicates the image, sometimes making the native image plane, originally hidden within a mechanical holder, accessible. The general relay system can have a magnification factor other than one. Below are some observations about the relay system:

4f System

A 4f system uses a pair of lenses ($ f_1, f_2 $) with the back focal plane of the first lens co-localized with the front focal plane of the second lens. This co-localized plane is known in Fourier optics as the Fourier plane, as it performs an optical Fourier transformation of the object. Unlike a single lens relay, a 4f system does not introduce phase errors to the relayed image, thus replicating the target complex field perfectly[1]. That is to say that it relays both the location and angle of the rays.

A 4f system cannot be reduced to a single lens equivalent. In addition to the phase error mentioned earlier, the total focal length is effectively ‘infinite’.

One interesting observation is that, the maginification of 4f system is always $-f_2/f_1$. In the special case where $f_1=f_2$, the total working length (from the original image to the relayed image) will also maintain $2f_1+2f_2$, regardless of the image location. This property can be proved using Gaussian equations:

$$
\frac{1}{l_1} + \frac{1}{l_1’} = \frac{1}{f_1}
$$
$$
\frac{1}{l_2} + \frac{1}{l_2’} = \frac{1}{f_2}
$$
therefore,
$$
l_2’ = \frac{l_2f_2}{l_2-f_2} , l_1 = \frac{l_1’f_1}{l_1’-f_1}
$$
and the system magnification is
$$
M = \frac{l_2’l_1’}{l_2l_1} = \frac{f_2}{l_2-f_2}\frac{l_1’-f_1}{f_1}
$$
Because $ l_1’ + l_2 = f_1 + f_2 $, we can rewrite $ l_1’-f_1 = f_1+f_2 - l_2 - f_1 = f_2 - l_2 $.
Thus, the magnification is reduced to
$$
M = -\frac{f_2}{f_1}
$$
This magnification is constant regardless of $l_1$ and $l_2$.

And we can also derive that
$$
l_1+l_2’ = \frac{l_2f_2}{l_2-f_2} + \frac{l_1’f_1}{l_1’-f_1} = \frac{l_2f_2}{l_2-f_2} - \frac{(f_1+f_2-l_2)f_1}{l_2-f_2}=\frac{l_2-f_1}{l_2-f_2}(f_1+f_2)
$$
therefore,
$$
l_1+l_1’+l_2+l_2’ = 2*(f_1+f_2), when \ f_1=f_2
$$

Another advantage is that we can build a telecentric relay system for both the object and image space. This is ideal for numerical aperture (NA) coupling between front-end optics and subsequent systems behind the relay.

In some array-based optics, such as Fourier light field imaging, where an array of lenslets is used to form sub-aperture images, the image space consists of multiple parallel image channels. A 4f system maintains not only the positions of these channels but also their propagation direction. Parallel ray bundles remain parallel after passing through the relay.

Alignment

An exact 4f system can be aligned by finding the front/back planes of each lens sequentially.

When aligning the back focal planes of lens 1 and the front focal plane of lens 2, one can place a pinhole between them. Project illumination towards first lens through the pinhole and apply a mirror behind the target lens. Adjust the pinhole location. If the reflected light forms a shape pinhole image on pinhole plane, the pinhole is at the focus. Don’t move the pinhole. Move the illumination to the other side and place a mirror behind the second lens. Adjust the position of second lens this time until a sharp image is found on pinhole plane. Then the co-localized focal plane is achieved.

However, in most cases, a rough alignment, for example, where we use a ruler to control the lens spacing to be $f_1 + f_2$, is enough.

Finding focal plane of a lens

  1. Place a mirror behind the lens
  2. Translate a transmissively illuminated pinhole in front of the lens until the reflected light forms a sharp pinhole image. The location of pinhole is the front focal plane of the lens.

Dual lens with spacing other than $f_1+f_2$

It’s not a 4f system anymore. And it can be equivalent to a single lens. For example, for two 200 mm focal length lens with spacing of 200 mm, we can use the gaussian reduction to calculate the total optical power $\psi$ :

$$
\psi = \psi_1 + \psi_2 - \psi_1\psi_2\tau = 1/200 + 1/200 -1/200 * 1/200 * 200 = 1/200 mm^{-1}
$$

which is the same as a single 200 mm focal length lens.

The special case, where the lens spacing is exactly $f_1$, can be used to minimize vignetting if we have telecentric object space and if the second lens has to have a small aperture. It is because we force the chief ray to be centered by putting the stop, i.e. the second lens at the back focal plane of the first lens. Thus, the second lens blocks the same amount of light for every point of the FOV.

By placing the object at the front focal plane of the first lens, it is able to create ‘infinite space‘ within the relay, which is similar to 4f system. It becomes convenient to insert glass plate, filter, beamsplitter, grating, etc. without worrying about image plane shift.

Compared to single lens, it’s easier to align because:

  1. Magnification can be controlled by simply switching focal length pair, rather than tuning both object and image distance;
  2. Object position can be adjusted and be brought to focus without moving the image plane. In other words, the first lens can be moved without the necesity of moving the second lens correspondingly because the space in between is an infinite space.

Despite these convenience, dual lens relay normally introduces more aberration compared to single lens.

If we deviate from the preferred configuration that places the object at the front focal plane, then the magnification is no longer $f_2 /f_1$. And the infinity space is gone too.

Now we can discuss it just like its single lens equivalence:

First, the magnification is adjustable by changing object/image distance. And the total system length will also change during this adjustment. Using gaussian equations, one can find that the minimal total length will be obtained while the maginification is 1.

If the lens has a focal length $f$, let’s denote the object and image space as $l$ and $l’$. We have
$$
\frac{1}{l} + \frac{1}{l’} = \frac{1}{f}
$$

Thus
$$
l’ = f + \frac{f^2}{l-f}
$$

When $ l > f $, $ l’ $ is also larger than $ f $ and forms a real image. In the figure below, the shaded area marks the region of possible object/image distances constrained by the max total length. When the max total length is large, the achievable maginification, represented by the purple line, can also be large. When the maginification is 1, the total length reaches the minimum $ 4f $.

A conclusion can be that, the larger the total system length is, it creates a larger space for us to tune the magnification. And no matter how we adjust it, the minimal length is $ 4f $, and that’s when object distance equals to image distance, and maginification being 1.

Relay the aperture plane

Sometimes, we want to relay the aperture, rather than an image. For example, we want to place a component at the exit pupil of a microscope objective. A relay can thus extend that pupil plane to an accessible location.

In most cases, a 4f system will be the easiest solution as it replicates both the location and angle of the light. And the latter can be critical for pupil relay.

However, a single lens relay is also sometimes adopted. For example, in the camera array based light field microscopy [2], a single short focal length lens creates large maginification of the aperture plane of the microscope. Therefore the entire camera array can fit.

When using single lens for aperture relay, the magnification is more flexible than 4f system. However, it requires extra consideration on the choice of focal length and distance. For example, in the aforementioned camera array system, the single lens will create an intermediate image plane at the back focal plane. If placing the following lens too close to the relay, such as within the focal length, it may not be able to form an image.

References

[1] Wade T. Cathey. Comparison of Single-Lens and Two-Lens Coherent Imaging of Complex Distributions

[2] Xing Lin, Jiamin Wu, Guoan Zheng, and Qionghai Dai, Camera array based light field microscopy, Biomed. Opt. Express 2015
